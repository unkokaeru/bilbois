## What is a Complex Number?

Defined as $z=x+iy$, where $i^{2}=-1$ and $x,y \in\mathbb{R}$ - often these are called **real** ($\text{Re}(z) = x$) and **imaginary** ($\text{Im}(z) = y$) parts.

### What operations do they have?

This set of numbers, $\mathbb{C}=\left\{ x+iy:x,y\in \mathbb{R} \right\}$, is defined as a **field** because of the following operations...

- **Addition**: $z_1 + z_2 = (x_1 + x_2) + i(y_1 + y_2)$, *this also implies scalar multiplication*.
- **Multiplication**: $z_1 z_2 = (x_1x_2 - y_1y_2) + i(x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)$.
- **Division**: Multiply by **conjugate** $\overline{z} = x - iy$; result scaled by $|z|^2$ (**modulus**: $|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$).

### What properties do they have?

They have two main properties:

- **Triangle Inequality**: $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$, $|z_1 + z_2| \leq |z_1| + |z_2|$.
- **Part Separation**: $z + \overline{z} = 2\text{Re}(z)$, $z - \overline{z} = 2i\text{Im}(z)$.

However, since complex numbers are a **two-dimensional field**, they can be plotted on an **Argand diagram** (x-axis being the real part, y-axis being the imaginary part). This often done in polar coordinates after converting to **polar form**...

$$
z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta):r = |z|,\theta = \arg(z)
$$

Using **Euler's Formula**, $e^{i \theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta$, we can convert complex numbers in this form into **Exponential Form**, $z = re^{i\theta}$. **Euler's Identity** is also sometimes useful with related topics: $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$.

#### **Example**: Using properties to solve complex algebraic equations

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Solve } Z^3 &= 8i \\
\text{Express } 8i \text{ in polar form: } & \quad 8i = 8 \left( \cos \frac{\pi}{2} + i \sin \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 8e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}. \\
\text{General roots: } Z &= \sqrt[3]{8} \cdot e^{i\left( \frac{\pi/2 + 2\pi k}{3} \right)}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2. \\
\text{Simplify: } Z &= 2e^{i\left( \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{2\pi k}{3} \right)}. \\
\text{Roots: } 
k = 0: &\quad 2 \left( \cos \frac{\pi}{6} + i \sin \frac{\pi}{6} \right) = \sqrt{3} + i, \\
k = 1: &\quad 2 \left( \cos \frac{5\pi}{6} + i \sin \frac{5\pi}{6} \right) = -\sqrt{3} + i, \\
k = 2: &\quad 2 \left( \cos \frac{3\pi}{2} + i \sin \frac{3\pi}{2} \right) = -2i.
\end{aligned}
$$

We can simplify this process of finding the roots of complex numbers using **De Moivre's Theorem**: $z^n = r^n (\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta)$. With this, we can then calculate the $n$-th **roots of unity**, fairly simply:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Solve } Z^n &= 1. \\
\text{Write } 1 \text{ in polar form: } & \quad 1 = e^{i2\pi k} \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z}). \\
\text{Roots: } Z &= e^{i \frac{2\pi k}{n}}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1. \\
\text{Example (n=4): } & \quad Z = e^{i0}, \, e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}, \, e^{i\pi}, \, e^{i\frac{3\pi}{2}} = 1, \, i, \, -1, \, -i.
\end{aligned}
$$

These roots of unity form a **cyclic group** under multiplication, generated by a primitive root $\omega = e^{i\frac{2\pi}{n}}$. The group structure is $\{ 1, \omega, \omega^2, \dots, \omega^{n-1} \}$, where multiplying any two elements corresponds to rotating around the unit circle in the complex plane.

To prove **De Moivre’s Theorem** for $z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$ and integer $n$, we use induction and Euler’s formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Base case (n=1):} \quad & z^1 = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta). \quad \text{True.} \\
\text{Assume true for \( n = k \):} \quad & z^k = r^k (\cos k\theta + i \sin k\theta). \\
\text{Prove for \( n = k+1 \):} \quad & z^{k+1} = z \cdot z^k = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta) \cdot r^k (\cos k\theta + i \sin k\theta) \\
& = r^{k+1} \left[ \cos \theta \cos k\theta - \sin \theta \sin k\theta + i (\sin \theta \cos k\theta + \cos \theta \sin k\theta) \right] \\
& = r^{k+1} \left[ \cos (k+1)\theta + i \sin (k+1)\theta \right]. \quad \text{(Trigonometric identities)} \\
\text{Conclusion:} \quad & \text{By induction, } z^n = r^n (\cos n\theta + i \sin n\theta) \text{ for all } n \in \mathbb{N}. \\
\text{Extension via Euler:} \quad & z = re^{i\theta} \implies z^n = r^n e^{in\theta} = r^n (\cos n\theta + i \sin n\theta). \quad \square
\end{aligned}
$$
